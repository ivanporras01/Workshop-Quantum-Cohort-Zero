Why light matters

See and identify matter

Visible light lets us see. A spectrometer separates light by wavelength so we can identify atoms from the lines they emit or absorb. These four lines are examples from hydrogen.

410, 434, 486, and 656 nm

Carry information

Infrared light carries signals through optical fiber. In a quantum link, one photon can carry a qubit in two polarization states. Polarization describes the orientation of the light’s electric field.

Control and read qubits

Laser pulses can change the states of trapped atoms. A detector can read some atomic qubits by seeing whether they fluoresce. Superconducting qubits use another part of the same spectrum: microwaves.

What we can demonstrate at the bench

Amplitude · change the height

With one BNC cable, change Vpp and hold frequency fixed. The trace gets taller. For a calibrated resonant qubit drive, pulse strength and duration help set the rotation angle. A quantum state amplitude is a complex coefficient, and its squared magnitude gives an outcome probability in the chosen basis.

Frequency · count cycles

Set 1 kHz, then 2 kHz. One cycle shrinks from 1 ms to 0.5 ms. A drive near a qubit’s transition frequency couples its two energy levels, whose gap is ΔE = hf. The bench compares timing at kilohertz. Superconducting qubit drives typically use gigahertz microwaves.

Phase · compare two traces

Use two synchronized channels at the same frequency. At 2 kHz, a quarter cycle is 125 µs (90°). Equal sine waves add at 0° and cancel at 180° in the scope’s sum. In a resonant qubit control model, drive phase selects the rotation axis in the XY plane. Relative state phase can change interference outcomes, as the H → Z → H guide shows.
